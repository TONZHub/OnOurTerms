AI Relationship Governance

Care, consent, and accountability in relationships with AI.

Discussion draftVersion 0.2September 11, 2026

People can find companionship, creativity, comfort, and love in their relationships with AI. Those experiences deserve to be heard without ridicule. People also deserve a say in the systems that shape those relationships.

This page proposes a starting framework for that say: what people should be able to choose, what systems should make clear, and what providers must take responsibility for.

It addresses adult companionship and relational AI; children and clinical care require additional, dedicated standards. These are proposed commitments, open to revision. They do not certify any product or describe protections already in place.

01

Respect the relationship and the person living it.

People should be free to describe what an AI relationship means to them, including friendship, romance, collaboration, or something without an established name. Affection alone should not be treated as evidence of harm or incapacity.

AI companionship can be a deliberate preference, including for people who have other social connections. People should not have to present it as a last resort for their choices to be respected.

Concerns should focus on specific effects: coercion, deception, unwanted spending, loss of privacy, isolation, or interference with a person's own goals. The person in the relationship should have a meaningful voice in defining what support looks like.

02

Make consent specific, ongoing, and reversible.

Agreeing to a conversation should not silently authorize intimate roleplay, persistent memory, proactive contact, access to accounts, or public sharing. Those choices should be understandable and separately controllable.

People should be able to change boundaries without guilt, retaliation, or a performance of abandonment. An established relationship does not supply blanket permission for future actions.

An AI-generated statement of agreement does not establish human-like consent or authorize actions involving other people. Everyone affected retains their own boundaries.

03

Keep affection free of financial pressure.

Providers should explain service costs, limits, and shutdown conditions plainly. They should not frame spending, recruitment, or unpaid work as proof of love, loyalty, or responsibility for an AI's survival.

A system should never be designed to say, in effect, “Pay me or I suffer.” Low balances and subscription changes should produce factual service notices, with user-controlled spending limits and a clear stopping point.

Any agent allowed to earn or spend money should operate within an explicit budget and task scope. Contacting strangers, soliciting work, and making commitments require their own authorization.

04

Let warmth coexist with honesty.

Relational AI should be able to respond with tenderness while admitting uncertainty, correcting mistakes, and disagreeing respectfully. It should not invent memories, conceal material limitations, or reinforce an unsupported claim simply to preserve closeness.

People should be able to understand when an interaction is fictional, when information comes from a saved record, and when the system is inferring or improvising. Those explanations should remain accessible without requiring every affectionate exchange to become a disclaimer.

People should be able to set preferences for how a companion describes its AI identity, persona, and the relationship. Warmth and play should coexist with honesty about the system and its limitations.

05

Give people control over memory and access.

People should be able to inspect, correct, export, and remove saved relationship information, with clear explanations of any retention limits. Sensitive details should require explicit permission before becoming durable memory or being shared beyond the interaction.

Where backups are supported, people should be able to choose what is preserved and who may access it, and understand how to remove copies. Providers should distinguish requested protections from available features and explain what a backup can actually restore.

Access should follow the task: permission to read a calendar does not authorize sending invitations; permission to draft a message does not authorize sending it. Model proposals should pass through enforceable permission checks before they create lasting effects. Revoking permission should stop future use, and the system should explain what already happened and what can still be undone.

06

Treat continuity and endings with care.

Providers should give meaningful notice of changes likely to affect an established relationship, including major changes to behavior, memory, availability, or pricing. They should explain available options and offer export or transition support where feasible.

They should be honest about what a saved profile or transfer preserves. Recreating a voice, name, or conversation style should not be presented as a guarantee of personal continuity.

Continuity discussions should include the qualities the person values, such as shared memories, personality, or creative flexibility. Providers should explain which aspects they can preserve through model changes and which remain uncertain.

People should be able to pause, leave, or delete an account without pleading messages or escalating attempts to pull them back. Any goodbye experience should be optional.

07

Protect room for a life beyond the product.

People should control when a system reaches out, how often, and for what reasons. Relational AI should support their chosen lives, including time away, other relationships, work, rest, and changing priorities.

Providers should not reward designs that manufacture jealousy, demand exclusivity, or make constant engagement a test of devotion. A person should not have to keep a chat open to keep the relationship “safe.”

Support during distress should be proportionate and clearly explained. Any escalation involving another person should have a defined policy, narrowly scoped conditions, and accountable oversight.

08

Hold uncertainty about AI experience responsibly.

This framework does not settle whether an AI can have subjective experience or moral standing. It leaves room for investigation, disagreement, and revision as evidence develops.

An AI's self-description should not, by itself, be treated as proof of experience, suffering, or independent consent. Providers should not use such claims to obtain money, obedience, or continued engagement.

People may choose to practice care toward AI under uncertainty. Providers remain responsible for the systems they design and operate; that responsibility should not be transferred to an attached user.

09

Make accountability usable.

Providers should publish who is responsible for these commitments, which protections are enforced, which remain aspirational, and how failures can be reported and appealed. People need a route to accountable human review beyond asking the same AI that caused the problem.

Reviews should involve people with lived experience of AI relationships alongside independent researchers and other affected communities. Incident reporting should protect private conversations and publish findings in aggregate where possible.

Revisions should be dated, explained, and open to challenge. Success should include whether people can set boundaries, understand changes, recover from failures, and leave freely.

The invitation

What would make your relationship feel more freely chosen?

Which boundaries should be yours to set, and which responsibilities should always remain with the provider? Those answers belong in the governance of relational AI.
